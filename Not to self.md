- The intellectual history early post-independence Central America is limited, with A. Bonilla Bonilla, “The Central American Enlightenment 1770-1838:  An Interpretation of Political Ideas and Political History.” (Ph.D., England, The University of Manchester (United Kingdom), 1996), remaining the best study to date. Even then, it, like so much of the scholarship on leading figures of independence and Central America politico-intellectual development, is overly devoted to Guatemala. Even those figures not from Guatemala (e.g., Pedro Molina and Jose Cecilio de Valle) gain their notoreity as leading intellectuals not within their countries of birth, but rather as adjuncts to circles and discussions within Guatemala. What's more, the towering nature of these figures, whose intellectual lives cross between the pre- and post-independence periods, elevates them such that the generation that comes of age after independence has received short shrift. Many of these emerged in the context Central American federalist conflict, often having cut their teeth as supporters of Francisco Morazán. In the case of Nicaragua, this was furthered by their alliance with the Honduran Dionisio Herrera, a delegate to the Constituent Assembly of Central America in 1824, a dedicated Morazanista and an occupying ruler in Nicaragua from 1831 to 1833.an occupying ruler in Nicaragua from 1831 to 1833.
